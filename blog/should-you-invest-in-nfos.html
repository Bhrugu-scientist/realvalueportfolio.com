Should You Invest in NFOs (New Fund Offers)?

By Bhrugu Thakkar · Real Value (ARN 24454) · September 2026 · 6 min read
Short answer: Ignore the "₹10 unit, so it's cheap" pitch entirely — that's marketing, not maths. Invest in an NFO only if it offers something genuinely new that fills a real gap in your portfolio, run by a house with a credible track record in that space. Otherwise, an existing fund with 5–10 years of proven history is almost always the safer bet.

Every few months a new fund launches with posters showing a rocket, a ₹10 NAV, and a limited subscription window. The pitch sounds urgent: "Get in early, before the price goes up." It's one of the most persistent myths in Indian retail investing — and it's worth unpacking properly.

The ₹10 NAV myth, killed with one example

NAV (Net Asset Value) is not a price tag like a stock. It's simply: total fund assets ÷ number of units. A new fund starts at ₹10 because that's an arbitrary starting point, not because it's undervalued.

Identical outcome. The number of units you hold and the NAV per unit are irrelevant to your returns — only the percentage change matters. A ₹10 fund is not cheaper, and a ₹4,000 fund is not expensive.

The real risk with NFOs: no track record

The actual issue with an NFO has nothing to do with price and everything to do with information. When you buy an existing fund, you can check:

  • How it performed across at least one full market cycle (a rally and a fall)
  • How its returns compare to its category average and benchmark
  • Portfolio turnover, expense ratio trends, and fund manager consistency
  • How it behaved in the worst months — 2020's crash, for instance

With an NFO, none of this exists yet. You are trusting a Scheme Information Document, a fact sheet of intentions, and the fund house's reputation — nothing more. That's a real, measurable disadvantage, unrelated to the NAV.

When an NFO can genuinely make sense

Not every NFO is marketing fluff. A small number fill real gaps:

  • A new index or theme unavailable elsewhere — e.g., access to a specific market segment or factor strategy with no existing comparable fund
  • Run by a house with proven expertise in that exact mandate — a fixed income specialist launching a new debt category fund, for example
  • Genuinely first-to-market in India for a strategy that has a long track record abroad or in institutional portfolios

In these cases, you're not betting on an unknown manager's stock-picking skill from scratch — you're accessing new exposure through a credible operator.

A simple three-question filter

  1. Does this fund do something no existing fund does? If ten similar funds already exist with 5+ year records, why choose the untested one?
  2. Does the fund house have credibility in this specific mandate? A strong equity house launching its first debt fund is a different risk than an established debt manager doing the same.
  3. Would I still want this exposure if it weren't being marketed to me right now? If the answer is no, the urgency is doing the selling — not the fund.

The honest verdict

For most goals, a fund with a real track record — one you can actually evaluate against a benchmark and category — is the more prudent choice than an NFO riding on launch-week marketing. Reserve NFOs for the rare cases where the strategy is genuinely unavailable elsewhere and the fund house has demonstrated skill in that exact space. If you're building a long-term portfolio, understanding how funds and share classes actually work and how to evaluate a fund properly will serve you far better than chasing the next ₹10 launch.
