Can NRIs Invest in Indian Mutual Funds?

By Bhrugu Thakkar · Real Value (ARN 24454) · October 2026 · 7 min read
Short answer: Yes, NRIs can invest in Indian mutual funds — through an NRE or NRO account, after KYC with FATCA declaration. The process is simple for most countries, but US and Canada-based NRIs face extra restrictions since many AMCs limit or decline their applications.

If you've moved abroad and still want your money growing in India — in rupees, in funds you understand, for goals back home — the good news is it's entirely legal and fairly straightforward. Here's exactly how it works.

Step 1: Get the right bank account

You cannot invest using a regular resident savings account once your status changes to NRI. You need one of these, linked under FEMA (Foreign Exchange Management Act) rules:

Account typeSource of fundsRepatriation
NRE (Non-Resident External)Foreign income remitted to IndiaFully repatriable — principal + returns
NRO (Non-Resident Ordinary)Indian income — rent, dividends, old FDsUp to USD 1 million/year after tax, with CA certificate

Your mutual fund investment is tagged to whichever account you invest from, so choose based on where the money originates and whether you'll want to pull it back abroad later.

Step 2: Complete NRI-specific KYC

This is where most NRIs get stuck — not because it's hard, but because nobody explains it clearly. You'll need:

  • PAN card (mandatory, no exceptions)
  • Overseas address proof (utility bill, bank statement, or notarised ID)
  • Indian address proof, if any
  • Passport and visa/PIO/OCI copy as applicable
  • In-person verification (IPV) — often done via video KYC now, so you rarely need to fly down
  • FATCA/CRS self-declaration — this one is non-negotiable for every NRI, every country

Step 3: The US/Canada catch

Here's the part most articles skip. The US's FATCA law puts heavy reporting obligations on any financial institution accepting US-connected clients. Many large Indian AMCs decided it wasn't worth the compliance cost and simply stopped onboarding new NRI investors from the US and Canada.

A smaller set of fund houses still accept US/Canada NRI money, sometimes with extra paperwork or only through certain route (like a Power of Attorney held by a resident Indian). If you're based in the US or Canada, you must check AMC-by-AMC acceptance before applying — don't assume every scheme is open to you.

Step 4: Taxation — what actually gets deducted

NRIs are taxed the same way as residents on capital gains, but with TDS deducted upfront:

Fund typeHolding periodTax treatment
Equity funds> 1 year (LTCG)12.5% above ₹1.25 lakh gains/year
Equity funds< 1 year (STCG)20%
Debt fundsAny periodTaxed at slab rate (post-2023 rules)

The key difference from resident investors: TDS is deducted at source on redemption, at the applicable rate, before the money even reaches your account. If your actual tax liability is lower (due to the India-your-country DTAA), you claim the difference back while filing your Indian tax return.

Step 5: Investing itself — SIP, lumpsum, or NPS-style long-term

Once KYC and the bank account are sorted, investing works exactly like it does for resident Indians — SIPs, lumpsums, switches, and redemptions, all through standard mutual fund platforms or a distributor who can process NRI-specific forms correctly. The investment process doesn't change; only the onboarding and tax deduction do.

Common mistakes NRIs make

  • Investing from a resident account that wasn't converted after moving abroad — this is a FEMA violation, not just a technicality
  • Picking a fund house blind, only to find out later it doesn't accept US/Canada NRI redemptions or fresh SIPs
  • Ignoring DTAA and overpaying tax in both countries unnecessarily
  • Letting PAN or KYC lapse when address or status changes, which freezes folios until re-verified

The honest verdict

NRIs absolutely can — and many profitably do — invest in Indian mutual funds. The returns potential, rupee exposure for India-based goals, and tax efficiency versus some foreign-domiciled options make it genuinely worthwhile. The friction is entirely in the paperwork: right account, right KYC, right AMC if you're from the US or Canada. Get that right once, and the ongoing investing is no different from any resident investor's.
