US / Canada NRI Mutual Fund Rules: The FATCA Problem (2026)

By Bhrugu Thakkar · Real Value (ARN 24454) · October 2026 · 7 min read
Short answer: It is legal for US and Canada-based NRIs to invest in Indian mutual funds, but FATCA compliance costs have made most AMCs quietly refuse new applications from them. A smaller list of AMCs still accepts US/Canada NRIs directly, and PMS, AIF, NPS, and direct equity remain open to everyone. The door isn't shut — it's just narrower.

If you're an NRI in New York, Toronto, or San Francisco and have tried opening a mutual fund folio in India recently, you've probably hit a wall: the application gets rejected at the KYC stage, or the AMC's website silently excludes your country of residence from the dropdown. This isn't a glitch. It's a direct consequence of a US law called FATCA. Here's exactly what's happening and what you can still do about it.

What FATCA actually is

The Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act (FATCA) is a US law requiring foreign financial institutions — including Indian AMCs — to identify US persons (citizens, green card holders, or tax residents) among their investors and report their holdings to the US IRS, via India's tax authorities. Canada has a similar information-sharing arrangement under CRS (Common Reporting Standard).

For the AMC, onboarding a US-linked investor means: extra due diligence, annual reporting obligations, legal exposure if something is missed, and ongoing compliance costs — all for what is usually a small slice of their investor base. Many AMCs did the cost-benefit math and simply stopped accepting new US/Canada NRI investors rather than build out full FATCA reporting infrastructure.

What this means practically

SituationWhat usually happens
New folio application, US/Canada NRIRejected by most (not all) AMCs at KYC stage
Existing folio opened before you moved to US/CanadaUsually continues to run; fresh lumpsum/SIP may still get blocked by some AMCs
SIP set up while resident in India, investor later moves to USOften continues, but check with the specific AMC — rules vary and have tightened
Switch or redemption on an old folioUsually permitted regardless of current residence

This is the part that causes the most confusion: there is no single blanket ban. It's a patchwork — some fund houses accept US/Canada NRIs with full FATCA paperwork, some accept only existing investors, and some have stopped entirely. The list changes, so always verify directly with the AMC or your distributor before assuming either way.

Options that remain genuinely open

  • AMCs that still onboard US/Canada NRIs — a shorter list, but real. They've invested in FATCA compliance infrastructure and will ask for a detailed FATCA/CRS self-certification plus additional KYC documents.
  • Portfolio Management Services (PMS) — structured differently from mutual funds, many PMS providers accept US/Canada NRIs, though minimum investment is typically ₹50 lakh.
  • Alternative Investment Funds (AIF) — similar higher-ticket route, Category II and III AIFs often accept US-linked investors with proper documentation.
  • NPS (National Pension System) — open to NRIs including US/Canada residents, no FATCA-based exclusion, good for long-term retirement-linked allocation.
  • Direct equity via NRE/NRO + PIS — buying listed shares directly doesn't carry the same AMC-level FATCA friction, though your US tax filing obligations (PFIC rules for mutual funds specifically) still apply.

The US tax side nobody mentions

Even where an Indian AMC accepts you, Indian mutual funds are typically classified as PFICs (Passive Foreign Investment Companies) under US tax law. PFIC taxation in the US is punitive and complex — potentially taxing gains at the highest marginal rate with interest charges, regardless of your actual US tax bracket, unless you make specific elections (QEF or mark-to-market) that require data most Indian AMCs don't provide in a usable format.

This is a bigger practical problem than FATCA account-opening friction for many US-resident NRIs. It's precisely why many choose NPS, direct stocks, or India-focused ETFs listed in the US instead of Indian mutual fund folios — not because they can't invest, but because the US tax drag can be worse than any return difference.

What to actually do

  1. Confirm your current tax residency status clearly — green card and substantial-presence-test rules in the US are not always obvious.
  2. If you already have Indian mutual fund folios from before you moved, don't assume they're safe — check with each AMC on your ability to continue SIPs or add lumpsum.
  3. Talk to a US tax professional about PFIC implications before investing meaningfully, not after.
  4. If mutual funds prove too restrictive, evaluate PMS, AIF, NPS, or direct equity for your India allocation.
  5. Keep your KYC and FATCA self-certification updated — outdated records are one of the most common reasons folios get frozen for NRIs.

The honest bottom line

FATCA didn't ban US and Canada NRIs from Indian mutual funds — it made the business case for serving them weaker for most AMCs, who responded by narrowing the door rather than widening their compliance team. The investing door is smaller, not closed. The real decision is less about finding an AMC that will take your money and more about whether a mutual fund folio, given PFIC tax treatment, is even the most efficient vehicle for your India exposure compared to PMS, AIF, NPS, or direct equity.
