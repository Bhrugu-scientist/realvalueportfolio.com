Recency Bias and Your Portfolio

By Bhrugu Thakkar · Real Value (ARN 24454) · October 2026 · 6 min read
Short answer: Recency bias makes you treat the last 1-2 years of returns as if they predict the future. They usually don't. The fund or sector that topped the charts recently is often the one most likely to underperform next, simply because it has already run ahead of its fundamentals.

Every investor has done it at least once: seen a fund or sector on top of the performance chart, felt the pull of "everyone's making money in this, why aren't I," and moved money in near the peak. That pull has a name — recency bias — and it is one of the most expensive habits in investing.

What recency bias actually is

Recency bias is a mental shortcut: we weigh recent, vivid information far more heavily than older, less exciting information. It helped humans survive in the wild — the last rustle in the grass matters more than one from six months ago. But markets don't work like survival instincts. A sector that rallied hard for two years is not more likely to keep rallying; often it is closer to the point where valuations have run ahead of fundamentals.

How it shows up in real portfolios

Exiting equity after a sharp correctionRecent painful headlines and red portfolio valueSelling near a cyclical bottom

Why it's so easy to fall for

  • Fund rankings reinforce it. Most "top funds" lists sort by 1-year or 3-year returns — exactly the window where recency bias is strongest.
  • Mean reversion is invisible until it happens. Categories and styles (large cap vs small cap, value vs growth, domestic vs global) rotate in and out of favour over cycles lasting several years. The outperformer of one cycle is rarely the outperformer of the next.
  • Loss aversion compounds it. A recent drop feels more urgent and personal than a long-term plan on paper, so recent pain overrides a calm, pre-decided strategy.

A simple test before you act on recent performance

  1. Pull up the same fund or category's performance over a full market cycle — ideally 7-10 years, including at least one downturn.
  2. Ask: is this recent outperformance explained by a specific, sustainable reason (e.g., a structural shift) or simply by being the latest thing to catch a rally?
  3. Check whether this move fits your original asset allocation, or whether you are abandoning your plan because of a headline.
  4. If you still want to act, size it small and treat it as a satellite bet — not a wholesale rebuild of your core portfolio.

Building a process that resists the bias

The antidote to recency bias isn't willpower — it's a written process you follow even when your gut disagrees. Decide your asset allocation and rebalancing rules when you're calm, not when a headline is loud. Review your portfolio on a fixed schedule (say, twice a year) instead of every time the market moves. And separate "informational noise" (this quarter's chart-topper) from "decision-worthy signal" (a genuine change in your goals, timeline, or risk capacity).

This ties closely into discipline around choosing the right instrument for each goal — recency bias often pushes people toward whichever option recently "felt" safest or most exciting, rather than what actually fits the timeline.

The honest verdict

Recency bias isn't a flaw unique to inexperienced investors — professional fund managers and seasoned investors fall for it too. The difference is that disciplined investors build guardrails: asset allocation targets, rebalancing bands, and a habit of checking full-cycle data before chasing short-term charts. Your portfolio doesn't need you to predict the next winner. It needs you to not abandon a sound plan because of what happened in the last twelve months.
