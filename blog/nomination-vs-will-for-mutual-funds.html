Nomination vs Will for Mutual Funds

By Bhrugu Thakkar · Real Value (ARN 24454) · October 2026 · 6 min read
Short answer: A nominee is not the owner — they are a trustee who receives the units from the AMC quickly after your death, purely to avoid delay. Who actually keeps those units is decided by your will, or by succession law if you have no will. Mismatched nominee and will = family dispute.

Most investors fill the nomination field once, during KYC, and never think about it again. Many assume "I've named my son as nominee, so the mutual fund is sorted." That assumption causes more family friction after a death than almost any other financial oversight. Let's separate the two concepts cleanly.

What nomination actually does

Nomination is an operational shortcut. When a unit holder dies, the Asset Management Company needs someone to hand the units to without waiting for courts or family consensus. SEBI rules allow the AMC to transmit units to the registered nominee on production of a death certificate and basic KYC — usually within a few weeks.

But the AMC's job ends there. The nominee is legally obligated to hold those units as a trustee for the rightful legal heirs or beneficiaries under the will. The nominee has no personal entitlement just because their name was on the form.

What a will actually does

A will is the legal document that decides who owns your assets after you die — mutual funds, property, bank balances, everything you choose to include. If your will says your mutual fund units go to your daughter, but your nominee on record is your brother, your brother receives the units from the AMC first — and is then legally required to transfer them to your daughter per the will.

In practice, this rarely happens smoothly. The brother may refuse, delay, or genuinely believe the units are his because "the AMC gave them to me." This is where otherwise avoidable litigation starts.

Common mistakes that cause disputes

  • Outdated nominee: You named an ex-spouse or a sibling years ago and never updated it after marriage or a change in family circumstances.
  • No nominee at all: SEBI now requires investors to either nominate or explicitly opt out in writing — opting out without a will leaves heirs facing a slow succession-certificate process.
  • Will and nomination contradict each other: Different people named in each, with no explanation, inviting a dispute neither document alone can resolve quickly.
  • Multiple folios, multiple nominees, one will: If your will doesn't clearly cover all folios and joint holdings, overlaps and gaps create confusion for the family.

How to actually get this right

  1. Review nominees across every folio whenever a major life event happens — marriage, divorce, a child born, a death in the family.
  2. Write a will that explicitly lists major financial assets, including mutual fund folios, demat accounts, and insurance. Don't leave it to be inferred.
  3. Keep nominee and will aligned wherever practical — it removes the single biggest source of post-death conflict.
  4. Tell your family where documents are. A perfect will locked in a drawer no one knows about delays everything just as much as not having one.
  5. Consider a letter of instruction alongside the will, listing folio numbers, registrars, and advisor contact details — it saves your family weeks of searching.

Why this matters more as your portfolio grows

When you're holding a handful of mutual fund units early in your investing life, the stakes of getting this wrong feel low. But as SIPs compound over 10–20 years into a meaningful sum, the nomination-vs-will gap becomes a real financial and emotional risk for the people you're investing for in the first place. Estate planning isn't a one-time form — it's maintenance, just like reviewing your SIP step-up or rebalancing your asset allocation periodically.

The honest verdict

Nomination is a convenience mechanism, not a succession plan. Treat it as the fast lane for paperwork, and treat your will as the actual legal truth of who owns what. Keep both updated, keep them aligned, and tell your family where to find them — that one afternoon of effort can save your heirs months of stress and legal cost later.
