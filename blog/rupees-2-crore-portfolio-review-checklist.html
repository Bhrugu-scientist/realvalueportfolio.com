What a ₹2 Crore Portfolio Review Should Cover

By Bhrugu Thakkar · Real Value (ARN 24454) · October 2026 · 7 min read
Short answer: A review at this size should go well beyond "how are my returns doing." It needs to check allocation drift, fund overlap, tax efficiency, nominee and estate details, withdrawal sequencing, and whether the number of funds has quietly become unmanageable.

Somewhere between ₹20 lakh and ₹2 crore, a portfolio changes character. It stops being a handful of SIPs you can track mentally and becomes something that needs an actual process — because the rupee cost of a mistake is no longer small. A ₹2 crore portfolio that's 5% misallocated is a ₹10 lakh problem, not a rounding error. Here's what a proper review should cover.

1. Asset allocation vs. the plan, not vs. the market

The first question isn't "did my portfolio go up." It's "did my equity:debt:gold mix stay where it was supposed to be?" A portfolio that started at 70:25:5 equity:debt:gold can silently drift to 82:13:5 after a two-year rally — simply because equity grew faster. That drift is often the single biggest risk hiding in a large portfolio, and nobody notices it until a correction hits.

CheckWhat to look for
Current allocationEquity / debt / gold / cash split, calculated on current value — not original investment
Target allocationWhat the plan said it should be, based on your goals and timeline
DriftAnything off by more than 5-8 percentage points usually needs rebalancing

2. Fund overlap — the hidden concentration risk

Portfolios this size often accumulate 12-18 funds over the years — one from every "hot" category, a few legacy holdings, some inherited advice. The review should check what's actually inside those funds. It's common to find that 10 funds across different names are all heavily weighted in the same 20-30 large-cap stocks. You think you're diversified; you're actually running one concentrated bet with extra expense ratios layered on top.

3. Tax position — realised and unrealised

  • How much long-term capital gains have already accrued, and what the tax bill would be if sold today
  • Whether gains can be harvested within the ₹1.25 lakh LTCG exemption each year without disturbing the strategy
  • Whether any funds are held in a way that creates avoidable short-term tax (frequent switching, wrong fund type for the goal)

At ₹2 crore, tax-smart exits and SIPs/STPs can realistically save lakhs over a decade — but only if someone is actually tracking the cost basis across every folio.

4. Goal mapping — is every rupee working toward something?

Large portfolios often mix money for very different purposes — retirement 20 years out, a child's education 6 years out, a house down payment next year — all sitting in the same pool of funds with no clear separation. A proper review maps each chunk of money to a specific goal and checks whether its current fund category actually matches that goal's time horizon. Money for a goal 2 years away sitting in a small-cap fund is a mismatch regardless of how good the fund has performed historically.

5. Number and quality of funds

More funds isn't more diversification past a point — it's more paperwork, more overlap, and more things to track on redemption. A well-run ₹2 crore portfolio can usually be managed cleanly with 6-10 funds across categories. If you're holding 15+, the review should identify which ones are redundant and can be consolidated — factoring in exit load and tax impact of each consolidation, not just doing it blindly.

6. Nomination, mode of holding, and estate basics

This is the section most reviews skip entirely, and it's arguably the most important at this size. Check that nominees are updated on every folio, that joint holding instructions are clear, and that whoever would need to claim this money in your absence actually knows these investments exist and how to access them. A ₹2 crore portfolio with no nomination trail is a ₹2 crore headache for your family later.

7. Withdrawal and sequencing plan

If any part of this portfolio will be drawn down in the next 5-10 years — for retirement income, a goal, or a lump sum need — the review should map out which funds get redeemed first, in what order, to minimise tax and avoid forced selling during a market dip. Without this plan, withdrawals tend to happen reactively, from whatever fund is easiest to access, which is rarely the most tax-efficient choice.

8. Cost check

Expense ratios, exit loads, and whether you're in direct or regular plans all compound meaningfully at this size. A 0.5% difference in ongoing cost on ₹2 crore is ₹1 lakh a year — worth checking line by line, not assuming it's fine.

The honest takeaway

A ₹2 crore portfolio review isn't a performance report card. It's a structural health check — allocation, overlap, tax, goals, estate, and cost, all looked at together. Most DIY reviews only check the first one (returns) and skip the other five, which is exactly where the expensive mistakes hide.
