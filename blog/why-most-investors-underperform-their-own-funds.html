Why Most Investors Underperform Their Own Funds

By Bhrugu Thakkar · Real Value (ARN 24454) · October 2026 · 7 min read
Short answer: A fund's published return assumes someone invested a lump sum on day one and never touched it. Real investors stop SIPs in corrections, chase last year's winners, and redeem in panic — so the average investor in a fund usually earns far less than the fund itself earned. This gap is called the behaviour gap, and it's bigger than any expense ratio you'll ever pay.

Here's a number that should bother every investor: a well-run equity fund can deliver 14% CAGR over ten years on paper, while the average rupee invested in that same fund earns closer to 8-9%. Same fund. Same decade. Wildly different outcomes — because the fund's return and the investor's return are not the same thing.

Two different numbers, often confused

MeasureWhat it assumesWhat it tells you
Fund return (TWR)One investor, invested from day one, untouchedHow well the fund manager did
Investor return (MWR)Real cash flows — SIPs, pauses, redemptions, re-entriesWhat you actually earned

The published return in a factsheet is almost always the first kind. Your bank statement reflects the second. The gap between them is the behaviour gap — and research across global and Indian markets consistently puts it at 3-5 percentage points a year for equity fund investors.

How the gap actually happens

  • Buying after the rally — money pours into a category after 2-3 good years, right when future returns are statistically likely to be lower.
  • Selling in the correction — the same investors panic and redeem after a 15-20% fall, locking in the loss right before the recovery.
  • Stopping SIPs when it hurts most — pausing a SIP during a downturn means missing the cheapest units of the entire cycle.
  • Chasing last year's topper — switching funds based on a 1-year rank list, not a process, means constantly buying what's already run up.
  • Checking NAV too often — daily tracking amplifies every dip into an emotional event, making panic decisions more likely.

A simple illustration

Imagine two investors in the same fund over 10 years, where the fund itself compounds at 13%:

InvestorBehaviourApprox. realised return
ASteady SIP, no pauses, ignored the noise~13% (close to fund return)
BPaused SIP in 2 downturns, added lump sum after 2 rallies~8-9%

Investor B didn't pick a worse fund. B picked the same fund and made worse decisions around it. Over 10 years on a ₹50,000/month SIP, that 4-5% gap can mean a difference of ₹25-40 lakh or more in the final corpus.

Why this matters more than fund selection

Most investors spend 90% of their decision-making energy picking the "best" fund and 10% on how they'll behave once invested. The data suggests this ratio should be reversed. A decent fund held with discipline for 10 years almost always beats a "great" fund held with panic and interruption. If you've read our piece on FD vs mutual fund, you already know equity needs time to work — the behaviour gap is simply what happens when investors don't give it that time.

How to close your own gap

  • Automate everything. A SIP that runs on autopilot removes the daily decision to invest or not.
  • Write down the goal and horizon for every investment before you start, so a market fall doesn't change your "why."
  • Review on a calendar, not on a chart. Check your portfolio quarterly or half-yearly, not every time the market moves.
  • Pre-decide your response to a 20% fall. If the plan says "continue SIP, maybe add more," write that down now, while you're calm.
  • Use an advisor as a behavioural circuit-breaker. The single biggest value an advisor adds isn't fund-picking — it's stopping you from making an emotional decision at the worst possible moment.

The honest takeaway

You cannot control what the market returns next year. You can almost fully control whether you behave like Investor A or Investor B above. Closing the behaviour gap — not finding a slightly better fund — is the single highest-leverage thing most investors can do for their long-term wealth.
