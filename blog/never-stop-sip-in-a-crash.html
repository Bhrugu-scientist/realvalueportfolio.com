Why You Should Never Stop a SIP in a Crash

By Bhrugu Thakkar · Real Value (ARN 24454) · October 2026 · 7 min read
Short answer: A crash is exactly when your SIP is buying the most units for the least money. Pausing it doesn't protect you — it simply guarantees you miss the cheap units and the recovery that usually follows.

Every market cycle, the same thing happens. The index falls 15-20%, portfolio statements turn red, and lakhs of SIP investors across India log into their apps and hit pause. It feels responsible. It feels like "waiting for things to settle." It is, almost always, the single most expensive decision a long-term investor makes.

What a SIP is actually designed to do

A Systematic Investment Plan isn't a bet that the market goes up every month. It's a mechanism that exploits volatility. Each month you invest the same fixed amount, and the fund allots units based on that day's NAV. When the market falls, the same ₹10,000 buys more units. When it rises, it buys fewer. Over a full cycle — fall, bottom, recovery — your average cost per unit ends up lower than if you'd invested a lump sum at the start. This is rupee cost averaging, and it only works if you keep investing through the fall, not just the rise.

The investor who paused months 2 to 5 invested only ₹20,000 and holds 200 units. The one who continued invested ₹60,000 and holds 736 units — almost 3.7x the units, bought mostly during the months everyone else was too scared to invest. When the market recovers, that difference compounds for years. This is the entire argument in one table.

Why your brain wants you to stop anyway

Pausing a SIP during a crash feels like control. Watching your portfolio value drop every day while you keep auto-debiting money into it feels like throwing good money after bad. But this is loss aversion playing tricks — your brain weighs the pain of a falling number far more heavily than the logic of unit accumulation. The investors who build real wealth are the ones who've trained themselves (or have an advisor who keeps them anchored) to see red months as a discount sale, not a warning siren.

When pausing might actually make sense

To be fair, there are legitimate reasons to stop a SIP that have nothing to do with market levels:

  • You've lost your income source and genuinely need the cash flow
  • The goal the SIP was funding has changed or disappeared
  • You're reallocating, not exiting — moving from one fund to a better-suited one after a proper review

"The market fell" is not on this list. If anything, a fall with your income and goals unchanged is a reason to consider a top-up, not a pause.

What to do instead when markets fall

  • Stop checking your portfolio daily — weekly or monthly is plenty
  • If you have surplus idle cash, consider a lump-sum top-up into the same funds
  • Revisit your asset allocation with an advisor, not your emotions
  • Remind yourself why the SIP exists — a goal 5-10+ years away doesn't care what the NAV did this week

The honest verdict

A crash doesn't damage a SIP investor's wealth — stopping the SIP does. The market doesn't send a notification when the recovery starts; it just happens, often fast and without warning, and the investors who benefit most are the ones who never left. Keep the SIP running. That's the whole strategy.
