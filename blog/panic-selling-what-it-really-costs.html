Panic Selling — What It Really Costs

By Bhrugu Thakkar · Real Value (ARN 24454) · October 2026 · 6 min read
Short answer: The market crash never costs you as much as your own exit does. A fall is a paper loss — it only becomes real and permanent the moment you sell. Most of the damage to investor returns comes not from markets, but from investors reacting to them.

Every few years the market falls 15-20% in a matter of weeks, headlines turn apocalyptic, and WhatsApp forwards start predicting the end of the financial system. Some investors hold on. Others sell everything, move to cash, and wait for "things to settle." The data on what happens next to each group is not close.

The difference between fund returns and investor returns

This is one of the most important and least discussed numbers in investing: the behaviour gap. A fund can deliver a perfectly respectable 12% CAGR over 10 years — and the average investor in that same fund can earn 7-8%, simply because of when they bought and sold. The fund didn't fail them. Their timing did.

This gap exists because money flows into funds after a rally (when everyone feels confident) and flows out during a crash (when everyone feels scared) — which is the exact opposite of what builds wealth.

What panic selling actually locks in

StageWhat happensYour position
Market falls 20%Portfolio value drops on paperLoss is temporary, unrealised
You panic and sellYou convert units to cash at the lowLoss is now locked in, permanent
Market recovers (often fast)Prices climb back, sometimes within monthsYou are in cash, missing the recovery
You re-enter, cautiouslyUsually after prices have already risenYou buy back higher than you sold

Notice what happened: you sold low and bought back high — the precise reverse of the one rule everyone claims to know.

Why missing the recovery is so expensive

Markets don't recover gradually and politely. A large chunk of a bull market's best days tend to cluster right after the worst ones — often while sentiment is still terrible and headlines are still gloomy. An investor who steps out during the panic and waits for things to "feel safe" again routinely misses these exact days, because by the time it feels safe, most of the recovery is already priced in.

Missing even a handful of the market's best days over a couple of decades can be the difference between a comfortable retirement corpus and a disappointing one. The cost isn't theoretical — it shows up directly in your final number.

What panic selling is really about

  • It's not a strategy, it's a reflex. Almost nobody panic-sells based on a calculated reassessment of fundamentals — it's an emotional response to a falling number on a screen.
  • It confuses volatility with loss. A fall in NAV is not a loss until you sell. Until then, it's simply the market doing what equity markets have always done.
  • It ignores your own timeline. If your goal is 10 years away, a 6-month crash is barely a blip on the chart you'll actually look back at.

What to do instead, when it actually hurts

  1. Check the goal, not the index. Has your actual financial goal changed, or just the market mood? Usually it's only the mood.
  2. Revisit why you chose the fund, not how it performed last month. If the reasons still hold, the fall is noise, not signal.
  3. If you must act, rebalance — don't exit. Trimming an over-allocated position is very different from liquidating in fear.
  4. Automate your SIPs through the fall. A crash is when your SIP quietly buys more units per rupee — stopping it defeats the entire purpose. See our note on SIP vs lump sum for how this plays out mathematically.

The honest verdict

Markets will fall again — that's not a risk, it's a certainty, and a known price of long-term equity returns. What determines your outcome isn't whether a crash happens, it's whether you have a plan written down before it happens, so the decision isn't made by fear in the moment. If you've ever been tempted to redeem everything during a bad week, it's worth reading about where short-term safety actually belongs in a portfolio — so panic has a smaller a role to play next time.
