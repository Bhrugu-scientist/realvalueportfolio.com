Legacy / Estate Planning Basics for Investors

By Bhrugu Thakkar · Real Value (ARN 24454) · October 2026 · 7 min read
Short answer: Building a portfolio is half the job. The other half is making sure it reaches the right people, smoothly, when you're not around. That means three things: correct nominees everywhere, a valid will, and family awareness of what exists and where.

Most investors spend years picking funds, tracking returns, and optimising tax — then leave the single most consequential document, a will, undone. Estate planning isn't morbid paperwork. It's the last and most important act of financial planning: making sure your effort actually benefits the people you built it for.

Nominee vs legal heir — the confusion that causes years of delay

This is the most misunderstood part of Indian investing. A nominee is not automatically the owner of the money after you. Courts have repeatedly held that a nominee is a trustee — someone who receives the asset to hand it over to the rightful legal heirs as per a will, or as per succession law if there's no will.

In practice, if your will clearly states who gets what, that governs. The nominee's job is only to make the transfer process faster with the fund house or depository — it's an operational convenience, not a legal verdict on ownership.

Why a will matters even for a "simple" family

  • Without a will, assets are divided per the succession law applicable to your religion (e.g., Hindu Succession Act), which may split your estate in ways you never intended — not necessarily to your spouse and children in the proportion you'd choose.
  • With a will, you decide exactly who gets what — including specific instructions for mutual funds, stocks, property, and even guardianship for minor children.
  • A simple will, properly signed and witnessed, is valid in India without registration — though registering it adds a layer of safety against disputes.

The practical checklist

  1. Update nominees everywhere — every mutual fund folio, demat account, bank account, insurance policy, and PF account. Many people nominate once at account opening and never revisit it after marriage, a child's birth, or a divorce.
  2. Write a will, even a simple one. List major assets, who inherits what, and name an executor you trust.
  3. Keep a master document (not the will itself, just a reference sheet) listing which AMCs, banks, insurers, and brokers you hold accounts with — so your family isn't searching blind.
  4. Tell at least one trusted person where the will and the master document are kept. A perfect will that nobody can find is as good as no will.
  5. Review every 3–5 years, or after any major life event — marriage, childbirth, property purchase, or a nominee's death.

What happens practically after death — the transmission process

For mutual funds, this is called transmission. With a valid nominee, the process is relatively simple: death certificate, KYC of the nominee, and a transmission request form to the AMC or registrar. Without a nominee, legal heirs typically need a legal heir certificate or succession certificate, an indemnity bond, and sometimes a no-objection from other heirs — a process that can stretch over months.

This is also where keeping your portfolio organised and trackable pays off twice — once for you while investing, and once for your family when they need to find and claim it.

A note on joint holding and "anyone or survivor"

Joint accounts with an "anyone or survivor" mandate let the surviving holder operate the account immediately without waiting for transmission paperwork. This is useful for liquidity but doesn't change actual legal ownership — that still flows through the will or succession law. Don't treat joint holding as a substitute for a will; treat it as a convenience layer on top of one.

The honest takeaway

Estate planning isn't about assuming the worst — it's about respecting the years of discipline that went into building the portfolio in the first place. A updated nominee list, a clear will, and a family that knows where to look turn a potentially painful, multi-month ordeal into a straightforward formality. It's one afternoon of paperwork that protects a lifetime of saving.
