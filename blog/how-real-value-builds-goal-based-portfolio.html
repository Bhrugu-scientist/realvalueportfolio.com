How Real Value Builds a Goal-Based Portfolio

By Bhrugu Thakkar · Real Value (ARN 24454) · October 2026 · 7 min read
Short answer: We don't start with funds — we start with your goals, list each one with its own time horizon and amount, decide the right asset mix for each, and only then pick categories and funds. The process is the same for every client; the numbers that go into it are yours alone.

Most people's "portfolios" are really just a collection of things they bought at different times — a SIP a cousin suggested, an ELSS fund bought in a tax-saving panic, an FD from a bank visit. Nothing is wrong individually, but together they don't add up to a plan. A goal-based portfolio flips this: every rupee is working toward something specific, with a timeline and a number attached.

Step 1: List every goal, separately

We start with a simple exercise — write down every financial goal you can think of, big or small, near or far. Not vague ones like "grow wealth", but specific ones:

GoalTime horizonToday's cost
Emergency fundImmediate6 months of expenses
Child's college12 years₹25 lakh (today's value)
Retirement22 yearsMonthly income needed
House down payment5 years₹15 lakh

This single step does more work than any fund selection ever could. A 5-year goal and a 20-year goal should never share the same investment — yet in most unplanned portfolios, they do.

Step 2: Convert today's cost into tomorrow's number

Every goal gets adjusted for inflation to its horizon. College costs don't rise at the general inflation rate — they often rise faster. We use goal-specific inflation assumptions rather than one flat number for everything, so the target you're saving toward is realistic, not optimistic.

Step 3: Match asset allocation to time horizon

This is the core of the process. Time horizon decides how much market risk a goal can absorb — not your personality, not what's trending, not what gave the best return last year.

  • Under 3 years: Mostly debt or liquid instruments. Capital protection first.
  • 3–7 years: A blend of equity and debt, with the equity share rising as the horizon lengthens.
  • 7+ years: Equity-heavy, because time is available to absorb volatility and compound through it.

Notice that none of this mentions a fund name. Asset allocation is decided before category selection, and category selection is decided before any specific scheme is discussed.

Step 4: Choose categories, not predictions

Once the equity-debt split is set for a goal, we move to fund categories — large cap, flexi cap, mid cap, short-duration debt, and so on — based on the role each needs to play. We check published category averages, expense ratios, and portfolio overlap rather than chasing last year's chart-topper. Our article on FD vs mutual fund covers why time horizon, not comfort, should drive this choice.

Step 5: Keep the number of funds sane

A goal rarely needs more than 3–4 funds. Owning eight funds across similar categories doesn't reduce risk — it usually just means you're holding the same top 20 stocks eight times over, with eight statements to track instead of one. We actively check for overlap before adding anything new.

Step 6: Set the review rhythm

A goal-based portfolio isn't "set and forget" — it's "set and review on schedule." We revisit:

  • Once a year, as a standard check-up
  • When a goal's horizon crosses a threshold (e.g., retirement goal moves from 10 years to 5 years away — time to shift allocation toward debt)
  • After major life events — job change, marriage, a new goal appearing
  • If the portfolio has drifted meaningfully from its target allocation due to market moves

Why this order matters

Most portfolio mistakes happen because the order gets reversed — people pick a "good fund" first and then try to find a goal to attach it to. By the time markets fall, that fund has no real purpose, so panic-selling becomes the default response. When every holding is tied to a goal and a horizon, a market dip is just noise for the goals that still have time — and a planned, calm shift for the goals that don't.

The honest bit

This process isn't unique or secret — any disciplined advisor should follow something similar. What matters is that it's followed consistently, revisited on schedule, and not abandoned the moment markets get volatile or a new "hot" fund appears. Structure beats inspiration, especially over 15–20 year goals.
