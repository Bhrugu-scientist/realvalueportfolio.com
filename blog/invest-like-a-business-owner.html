The Real Value Philosophy — Invest Like a Business Owner

By Bhrugu Thakkar · Real Value (ARN 24454) · October 2026 · 7 min read
Short answer: A mutual fund unit is not a lottery ticket or a number that moves on a chart — it's a claim on the earnings of real businesses. Investors who remember this stay invested through volatility, ask better questions, and build far more wealth than investors who trade on headlines.

Ask ten people why they bought a particular mutual fund and most will say something like "it gave 25% last year" or "my colleague suggested it." Almost nobody says "I looked at what businesses it actually owns and believed in their next ten years." That gap — between owning a number and owning a business — is the whole philosophy behind how we think about investing at Real Value.

What a business owner actually does

Think about someone who owns a family business — a textile unit, a pharmacy chain, a transport company. They don't check the implied resale value of their business every single day. They track things that actually matter:

  • Is revenue growing year on year?
  • Are margins holding up or getting squeezed?
  • Is the management reinvesting profits sensibly?
  • Is the industry itself growing, shrinking, or getting disrupted?

They ride out a slow quarter because they understand the business cycle. They don't panic-sell the shop because footfall dropped for three weeks during monsoon. That patience isn't blind faith — it's informed patience, built on understanding what they own.

Nothing here is about intelligence or effort — it's about which questions get asked. Change the questions, and behaviour changes automatically.

Why your fund unit really is a piece of a business

When you buy units of an equity mutual fund, the fund manager pools your money with thousands of other investors and buys shares of actual listed companies — the ones that make the two-wheelers on your street, the cement in your building, the medicines in your cabinet. You are, quite literally, a tiny part-owner of those companies through the fund.

The NAV moving day to day is just the market's mood swing about the value of those businesses — not a verdict on the businesses themselves. A business owner ignores the mood swings and watches the business. That's the single biggest behavioural shift this philosophy asks of you.

How this changes real decisions

  • During a market fall: a business owner asks "did the underlying companies' earnings power actually break, or did the price just drop?" Usually it's the latter — and that's often a buying opportunity, not an exit signal.
  • When choosing a fund: instead of only comparing 1-year returns, look at what the fund owns, how long the fund manager has run it, and whether the strategy matches your own time horizon.
  • When reviewing a portfolio: review it like a shareholder meeting — once or twice a year, calmly — not like a stock ticker you refresh out of anxiety.
  • When markets are euphoric: a business owner asks whether the price still makes sense relative to what the business actually earns, rather than chasing the crowd.

Patience is a strategy, not a personality trait

People often say "I'm just not patient enough to be a long-term investor," as if patience were something you're born with. In practice, patience is mostly a product of understanding. It's hard to hold through a 15% drawdown in something you don't understand. It's much easier to hold through the same drawdown in a portfolio of businesses whose quality you've actually examined. Understanding builds patience — not the other way around.

This is also why SIPs work so well with this mindset: a business owner who believes in the long-term story keeps adding capital through both good years and bad, because temporary price dips in good businesses are opportunities, not emergencies.

What this philosophy does not mean

It doesn't mean ignoring risk, blindly holding forever, or refusing to ever sell. It means your decisions — to buy, hold, add, or exit — should be anchored to the business and your own goals and time horizon, not to short-term price action or market noise. If your goal changes, if your time horizon shortens, or if a fund's strategy genuinely changes, that's a legitimate business reason to act. A scary news headline for one week is not.

If you're building this mindset from scratch, it helps to first get clear on the basics of how different asset classes actually behave over time, and to understand what return numbers truly mean before you judge any fund by them.

The takeaway

Every rupee you invest in an equity mutual fund buys you a sliver of real, working businesses — factories running, shops selling, engineers building. Respect that ownership the way a thoughtful business owner would: understand what you hold, judge it on fundamentals, and let time and patient capital do the compounding. The NAV is just noise around that signal.
