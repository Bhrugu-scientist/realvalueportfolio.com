Tax on International / US Funds: The Rules Indian Investors Keep Getting Wrong

By Bhrugu Thakkar · Real Value (ARN 24454) · September 2026 · 6 min read
Short answer: Since April 2023, gains from international and US-focused mutual funds are taxed entirely at your income tax slab rate — no matter how long you hold them, and with no indexation benefit. This is a very different — and often costlier — tax treatment than domestic equity funds.

A lot of investors added a US or global fund to their portfolio for diversification, saw the old tax rules quoted somewhere online, and are now surprised at redemption time. The rules changed. Here's what actually applies today.

What counts as an "international fund" for tax

The tax treatment isn't about the fund's name — it's about where the money is actually invested. A fund is taxed as "non-equity" for this purpose if it holds less than 65% in Indian equity shares on average. This covers:

  • US-focused funds (S&P 500, Nasdaq 100 feeder funds)
  • Global/international equity funds investing abroad
  • Fund-of-funds that invest in overseas mutual funds or ETFs
  • Gold funds, international REIT funds, and most debt funds

Domestic equity funds and most hybrid funds with 65%+ Indian equity are unaffected — they retain the older, more favourable equity tax treatment.

The tax rule, in plain terms

Domestic Equity FundInternational / US Fund
Holding period distinctionYes — 1 year cut-off (LTCG/STCG)None — same rate regardless of holding period
Tax rateSTCG 20%; LTCG 12.5% above ₹1.25L/yrAdded to income, taxed at your slab rate
Indexation benefitNot applicableNot available (removed from April 2023)
Exemption threshold₹1.25 lakh LTCG per yearNone

In effect: if you're in the 30% tax bracket, gains on your US fund are taxed at 30% (plus cess), whether you held it for 4 months or 8 years. There's no reward for patience built into the tax code here — the reward has to come from the fund's own returns.

A worked example

Suppose you invested ₹5,00,000 in a Nasdaq-linked fund three years ago and it's now worth ₹8,00,000 — a gain of ₹3,00,000. If your total taxable income puts you in the 30% slab:

  • Tax on this gain = 30% of ₹3,00,000 = ₹90,000 (plus applicable cess)
  • Compare this to the same ₹3,00,000 gain in a domestic equity fund held over 1 year: taxed at 12.5% on the amount above ₹1.25 lakh — a much smaller bill

The difference isn't small. It's a real drag on your net, after-tax return, and it should be factored into how much you allocate abroad, not just whether you allocate at all.

Why this changed

Before April 2023, funds with less than 65% domestic equity (including international funds and fund-of-funds) got debt-fund-like tax treatment with indexation after 3 years — often a better deal than pure equity taxation. The Finance Act 2023 removed indexation for these categories and pushed them to slab-rate taxation for all gains, aligning them with how debt funds are now taxed. International funds got swept into this change because structurally, they don't meet the 65% domestic equity threshold.

What this means for how you use these funds

  • Diversification case still holds — international funds still reduce single-country/single-currency concentration. The case is about correlation and currency, not tax efficiency.
  • Don't churn — since there's no holding-period tax benefit, frequent switching only adds transaction costs and tax events without any offsetting tax advantage.
  • Size the allocation with tax drag in mind — a 10-20% allocation to global equity for diversification is common; treat any higher allocation as a deliberate, tax-aware decision, not a default.
  • Track your slab — since these gains stack on top of your regular income, redeeming a large lump sum in a high-income year could push more of it into a higher slab. Spreading redemptions across financial years can help.

One thing people get wrong: foreign asset reporting

If you invest in a US fund through an Indian mutual fund (a feeder fund structure, which is how most retail investors access US and global markets), you hold units of an Indian scheme. You are not required to disclose this under Schedule FA (Foreign Assets) in your income tax return — that requirement applies to direct holdings of foreign shares, ESOPs, or foreign brokerage accounts, not domestic mutual fund units investing overseas.

The bottom line

International and US funds remain a legitimate diversification tool, but they're no longer a tax-efficient one. Go in with eyes open: slab-rate tax on every rupee of gain, no indexation, and no reward for holding long. Size the allocation for what it's meant to do — reduce concentration risk — and plan redemptions around your tax slab, not just market timing.
