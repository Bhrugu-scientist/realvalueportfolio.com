NRI Mutual Fund Taxation and TDS Explained (2026)

By Bhrugu Thakkar · Real Value (ARN 24454) · October 2026 · 7 min read
Short answer: NRIs investing in Indian mutual funds pay the same capital gains tax rates as residents, but with one major difference — TDS is deducted at redemption itself, often at a higher effective rate than your final liability. You can often claim back the excess by filing an Indian tax return, and DTAA may reduce your overall burden.

Many NRIs assume mutual fund taxation works the same way whether you live in Mumbai or Melbourne. It mostly does — same capital gains slabs — but the collection method is different, and that difference catches people off guard every redemption season. Here's how it actually works.

Capital gains tax rates: same as residents

The good news first — the underlying tax rates on mutual fund gains are identical for residents and NRIs:

Fund TypeHolding PeriodTax Rate
Equity funds (65%+ in equity)Up to 12 months (STCG)20%
Equity funds (65%+ in equity)Over 12 months (LTCG)12.5% above ₹1.25 lakh/year exemption
Debt funds (post-April 2023 rules)Any periodTaxed at slab rate as per current rules
Hybrid/other fundsDepends on equity allocationFollows equity or debt rule based on asset mix

So far, no surprise. The real difference for NRIs is in how this tax gets collected.

The TDS difference: deducted upfront, every single time

For resident Indian investors, mutual funds don't deduct any TDS on redemption — you calculate and pay your own capital gains tax while filing your return. For NRIs, the AMC is legally required to deduct TDS at the time of redemption itself, before crediting your bank account.

  • Equity LTCG: TDS typically at 12.5% on gains above the exemption threshold
  • Equity STCG: TDS typically at 20%
  • Debt fund gains: TDS at applicable slab-linked rates, often deducted at the highest applicable rate as a conservative default
  • Surcharge and cess: Added on top, which can push the effective TDS rate meaningfully higher than the final tax you actually owe

This means the amount credited to your NRO account after redemption is post-tax, not the full redemption value — unlike for residents who receive the gross amount and settle tax separately.

Why TDS often overshoots your real liability

TDS is calculated per transaction, in isolation. It doesn't know about:

  • Your ₹1.25 lakh annual LTCG exemption on equity funds, which may already be used up or still unused
  • Losses from other fund redemptions during the year that could offset this gain
  • Your total taxable income in India, which might be below the basic exemption limit
  • DTAA benefits your country of residence may entitle you to

As a result, many NRIs have more tax deducted than they actually owe. The fix isn't to avoid mutual funds — it's to file an Indian income tax return and claim the difference back as a refund.

How DTAA fits in

India has Double Taxation Avoidance Agreements with most countries NRIs live in — the UAE, USA, UK, Singapore, Canada, Australia, and more. Depending on the treaty, you may be entitled to a lower effective tax rate or a credit in your resident country for tax already paid in India.

In practice, DTAA usually doesn't reduce TDS deducted at source automatically — you still pay the TDS as levied, then:

  1. File your Indian tax return declaring the capital gains and TDS already deducted
  2. Claim applicable DTAA relief if your treaty allows a lower rate
  3. Claim a refund for any excess TDS versus your actual computed liability
  4. Separately, claim foreign tax credit in your resident country if required, using Form 67 and the Indian tax certificate

This requires a Tax Residency Certificate (TRC) from your country of residence and Form 10F, submitted to the AMC or RTA, which can sometimes reduce TDS at source if processed before redemption.

NRO vs NRE accounts and repatriation

Mutual fund investments by NRIs are usually routed through an NRO (non-resident ordinary) account, and redemption proceeds net of TDS are credited there. Repatriating funds abroad from an NRO account has its own compliance step — a CA certificate (Form 15CB) and a self-declaration (Form 15CA) are typically required for larger remittances. This is separate from the mutual fund TDS itself but often confused with it.

Practical checklist for NRI investors

  • Keep your KYC updated with correct NRI/NRO status — wrong categorisation causes TDS errors
  • Track your capital gains statement (most AMCs/RTAs provide a consolidated one annually)
  • File an Indian income tax return even if you think no additional tax is due — it's often the only way to recover excess TDS
  • Submit TRC and Form 10F proactively if you want treaty benefits considered before redemption
  • Work with a CA familiar with cross-border taxation — NRI tax filing has nuances that a generic preparer may miss

The honest takeaway

NRI mutual fund taxation isn't punitive — it's the same rates as residents, just collected differently. The TDS mechanism means your cash flow at redemption is lower than the headline return suggests, and getting the excess back requires an Indian tax filing, not a phone call to your fund house. Build that filing step into your annual routine, the same way you'd review your FD vs mutual fund allocation or rebalance your portfolio.
